\section{Introduction}

The increasing adoption of light weight materials and slender structural forms has made modern footbridges more susceptible to human-induced vibrations. 
As a result, vibration serviceability under pedestrian loading has become an important design consideration to ensure acceptable structural performance and user comfort.
Such assessment depends on several aspects of the bridge, including its structural form, material properties, and dynamic characteristics \cite{ahmadi2018framework,ahmadi2019assessment,ahmadi2021experimental}. 
Therefore, prior knowledge or reliable estimation of key modal parameters is essential at the design stage, as these parameters provide an initial basis for assessing the feasibility of a proposed design \cite{vzivanovic2007finite,van2014vibration,tadeu2022vibration,reynolds2025operational}.

The current vibration serviceability codes and guidelines represent pedestrian loading through simplified load models to enable practical application at the design stage \cite{BSI2008NAEN1991,EN1991_2_2023,JGJT441_2019,CJJ69_2017_draft,FIB2005Footbridges,setra2006,feldman2008hivoss}.
Since these load models are used to assess the vibration response of a wide range of footbridges, their adequacy should be evaluated in terms of the resulting structural response, rather than solely through how accurately they reproduce pedestrian loading.

The key limitation of many existing simplified load models is that pedestrians are represented as moving forces, with the corresponding load models calibrated without explicitly accounting for the dynamic interaction between the pedestrians and the supporting structure \cite{caprani2016formulation,shahabpoor2016interaction,van2017impact}.
Human--structure interaction (HSI) alters the dynamic properties of the coupled pedestrian--structure system, and can lead to structural responses that differ substantially from those predicted by simplified load models.
These effects can be particularly important for lightweight structures and for cases with relatively large pedestrian-to-structure mass ratios \cite{van2021simplified,van2017impact}. 
Consequently, simplified code models calibrated without accounting for HSI may exhibit systematic response bias that varies with the dynamic characteristics of the bridge, motivating their reassessment and response-based recalibration.

Response-based calibration provides such a framework by comparing the response predicted by the simplified design model with that obtained from experimental observations or a higher-fidelity reference model over a prescribed range of structural properties.
Previous studies have adopted this approach by evaluating simplified pedestrian loading or vibration serviceability models over ranges of natural frequency, damping and other structural parameters \cite{bassoli2018spectral,ahmadi2021experimental,van2017impact, van2021simplified,strobbe2026vibration}.
However, these structural domains considered in such studies have generally been defined using prescribed parameter ranges, idealised structural configurations, or regularly sampled parameter combinations rather than using the observed statistical distribution of dynamic properties within the footbridge population.
As a result, some structural conditions may receive disproportionate importance in the calibration simply because of the adopted sampling scheme, rather than because they are common in practice.

For code-model calibration, it is important to know not only the range of structural properties that may occur, but also how common these properties are within the footbridge population.
Several recent studies have made valuable efforts to compile databases of footbridge characteristics and modal properties. Oliveira et al. (2014) \cite{oliveira2014fundamental} reported fundamental frequencies for 79 footbridges in Portugal, while Van Nimmen (2015) reviewed data for more than 160 footbridges \cite{vanNimmen2015,zall2018}.
Wei et al. (2019) \cite{wei2019measured} assembled dynamic-property data for 138 footbridges, and Gong et al. (2024) \cite{gong2024basic} more recently studied a collection of footbridges in China.
These studies provide a substantial starting point, but their datasets were assembled for different purposes, report different properties, and include some of the same bridges.
Further observations are dispersed across many individual case studies \cite{vzivanovic2005human,tadeu2022vibration,lievens2016robust,strobbe2026vibration}.
Consolidating these records, identifying duplicate bridges, and recording which modal properties are available would provide a clearer basis for estimating their observed distributions.

This study addresses the above gap in two stages.
First, a systematic literature search and screening procedure following the PRISMA framework is used to identify relevant publications and compile bridge-level data on key modal properties.
Second, hierarchical Bayesian regression is used to probabilistically characterise incompletely observed modal properties while retaining the uncertainty associated with their estimation.
The resulting database provides a population-level description of key footbridge modal properties and their relationships, allowing the prevalence of different combinations of natural frequency, damping ratio and modal mass to be quantified.
Such information provides a statistical basis for distinguishing commonly occurring structural conditions from those that are comparatively rare.
This, in turn, can support the selection and weighting of representative structural parameter combinations used in vibration-serviceability assessment and code-model calibration.

Accordingly, this study aims to characterise the modal properties of the footbridge population using a systematically assembled bridge-level database of approximately 250 structures.
The principal contributions of the study are: (i) the systematic identification and consolidation of published footbridge dynamic-property data; (ii) the construction of a large, non-duplicated database of bridge-level modal properties; (iii) the probabilistic treatment of incompletely observed damping and modal-mass information using hierarchical Bayesian regression; and (iv) the population-level characterisation of modal properties relevant to vibration-serviceability assessment and code-model calibration.

\section{Systematic database development} 

The footbridge database was developed through a systematic literature identification and screening process following the PRISMA framework \cite{page2021prisma}.
The procedure includes publication identification, title and abstract screening, full text eligibility assessment, bridge level data extraction and consolidation of information into a single database.
The following subsections describe the search and screening procedure, the data extraction process, and the resulting database.

\subsection{Review scope and search strategy}

The systematic review aimed to identify published investigations reporting structural and dynamic properties of full scale pedestrian bridges.
The principal variables of interest were bridge span, deck width, funcdermental vertical natural frequency, vertical damping ratio, Modal mass, structural form, cross section, and construcion materials.

Scopus was selected as the literature database because of its broad coverage of peer-reviewed engineering research \cite{burnham2006scopus}.
The candidate publications were identified using Elsevier Scopus search API through title, abstract, and keyword feilds.
The search used a broader combination of pedestrian-bridge terminology with terms relating to natural frequency, damping, and structural mass, experimental dynamic testing, ambient vibration testing, operational modal analysis, model updating and vibration serviceability.

The search returned 727 bibilographic records before deduplication.
Dubplicate records were identified using DOI, Scopus EID.
Following deduplication, 697 unique records were retained for screening.
A DOI was available for 529 records, while Scopus EID were retained for publications without a DOI.
Scopus records were used to identify and screen publications, while numerical bridge properties were extracted from the full texts of the eligible studies.

\subsection{Eligibility criteria}

Publications were considered eligible when they reported an investigation of an existing, full-scale pedestrian bridge and contained experimentally measured or identified structural or modal properties that could be associated with a specific structure.
Studies based only on laboratory specimens, scaled models, hypothetical structures, or purely numerical investigations were excluded. 
Publications were also excluded where the reported frequency could not be identified as a structural vertical natural frequency relevant to the present study.
For inclusion in the bridge-level database, sufficient information was required to identify the bridge and extract its fundamental vertical natural frequency together with the geometric information required for the population analysis.
Damping ratio, modal mass, structural form, cross-section, and construction material were extracted where available but were not mandatory for inclusion.

\subsection{Study screening and bridge identification}

Following bibliographic deduplication, the 697 unique publications were retained for full-text screening. 
The full-text documents were processed using a semi-automated Python workflow.
PDF text was extracted using PyMuPDF and subsequently searched for evidence of full-scale pedestrian bridge investigations and relevant structural and modal properties.

The extracted text was searched for bridge names and terminology associated with natural frequency, damping, modal mass, mode shapes, and experimental vibration testing.
Reference sections were excluded from the search to reduce the likelihood of identifying bridges mentioned only in cited literature.

During the screening stage, each publication was counted as a single record, regardless of how many bridges it investigated. 
During data extraction, however, each individual bridge was treated as a separate database record. 
Consequently, a single publication could contribute data for several bridges.

Each detected bridge was assigned a separate candidate record containing its source publication, bridge identity, and associated structural and modal-property evidence. 
Candidate records were subsequently reviewed against the bridge-level inclusion requirements defined in Section 2.2 before consolidation.

\subsection{Bridge-level data extraction and consolidation}

For each candidate bridge, the extracted information included bridge name and location, span, deck width, fundamental vertical natural frequency, vertical damping ratio, modal mass, structural form, cross-section, and construction material. 
Numerical quantities were converted to consistent units before consolidation.

Because the same physical bridge could be reported in several publications, repeated bridge records were identified using bridge name, location, geometry, structural characteristics, reported modal properties, and source references. 
Potential matches across different publications were reviewed manually rather than being automatically removed.

Where several publications referred to the same physical bridge, the available information was consolidated into a single bridge-level record. 
The original experimental investigation was retained as the principal source where available, while additional information from later publications was incorporated where appropriate.

\subsection{Resulting database and data completeness}

Following bridge-level screening and duplicate reconciliation, 250 unique footbridges were retained in the final database.
Fundamental vertical natural frequency and span were available for all bridges, while deck width was available for 240 bridges.
Vertical damping ratios were reported for 100 bridges, whereas mass related information was considerably less complete, with modal mass information available for only 30 bridges and linear mass available for 25 bridges.
Structural form and material information were available for all bridges, while cross-section information was available for 242 bridges.
The resulting differences in data completeness, particularly for damping and mass-related properties, motivated the probabilistic treatment introduced in the following section.

\section{Bayesian treatment of incomplete bridge data}

The database contains complete records of fundamental vertical natural frequency and span, but damping and mass-related properties are reported for only a subset of bridges. 
Analysing only bridges with complete records would therefore exclude a substantial proportion of the database.
To retain these bridges in the population analysis, Bayesian regression models were developed to infer the unreported properties from the available bridge characteristics. 
The missing values were represented by posterior predictive distributions, allowing uncertainty in both regression parameters and remaining variability between bridges to be propagated into resulting population distributions. 

\subsection{Hierarchical model for vertical damping ratio}\label{sec:damping_model}

Let \(\xi_{i}\) denote the damping ratio of the first vertical mode for the \(i\)-th bridge.
The damping ratios \(\xi_i\) differ between bridges, reflecting differences in their construction materials, structural forms, and other factors.
However, the database does not provide sufficient details to model the physical mechanisms governing damping in each bridge.
Statistical modelling offers a means of estimating unreported values from observed data without explicitly identifying the underlying mechanisms \cite{mcelreath2018statistical,hwang2023deep,zhang2022bayesian}.
Accordingly, a Bayesian hierarchical regression model was fitted to the reported damping ratios, using natural frequency, span, and material as predictors that are available in the database.
The fitted model was then used to obtain posterior predictive distributions for unreported damping ratios, accounting for both uncertainty in the regression parameters and remaining variability between bridges.The probabilistic structure of the hierarchical damping model is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:damping_model}.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figures/fig_damping_model.pdf}
    \caption{Probabilistic structure of the hierarchical Bayesian
    model for vertical damping ratio.}
    \label{fig:damping_model}
\end{figure}

For each bridge, the available predictor information is \({x}_i=\{f_i,L_i,m_i\}\), where $f_i$ is the fundamental vertical natural frequency, $L_i$ is the span, and $m_i$ denotes the material category.
Let $\mathcal{O}$ and $\mathcal{M}$ denote the sets of bridges
with observed and missing damping ratios, respectively.
The data used to estimate the model parameters are then
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}
    = \{(\xi_i,\mathbf{x}_i):i\in\mathcal{O}\}.
\end{equation}

Damping ratios were modelled using a lognormal distribution to ensure positive predictions, with the corresponding log damping ratios assumed to follow a normal distribution:
\begin{equation}
    \log\xi_i \mid \eta_i,\sigma
    \sim \mathcal{N}(\eta_i,\sigma^2),
\end{equation}
where $\eta_i$ is the conditional mean of log damping ratio and $\sigma$ describes the remaining variability between bridges.
The conditional mean was expressed as
\begin{equation}
    \eta_i =
    \alpha+\beta_f x_{f,i}+\beta_L x_{L,i}+a_{m_i},
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is the common intercept, $\beta_f$ and $\beta_L$ describe the associations with natural frequency and span, and $a_{m_i}$ is the effect of the material category of bridge $i$.
The predictors $f_i$ and $L_i$ were logarithmically transformed and standardised as,
\begin{equation}
    x_{f,i} =
    \frac{\log f_i-\overline{\log f}}{s_{\log f}},
    \qquad
    x_{L,i} =
    \frac{\log L_i-\overline{\log L}}{s_{\log L}}.
\end{equation}
Here, the overbars and $s$ denote the means and standard deviations of the corresponding log-transformed predictors.

The material categories contain different numbers of observed damping ratios.
Estimating an independent model for each category would therefore provide limited information for sparsely represented materials.
Instead, material effects were assigned a shared distribution,
\begin{equation}
    a_m \mid \sigma_{\mathrm{mat}}
    \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{mat}}^2).
\end{equation}
This hierarchical structure allows information to be partially pooled across materials while retaining differences between categories.
The parameter $\sigma_{\mathrm{mat}}$ controls the variation in material effects around the common baseline.

Let $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ contain the regression coefficients, material effects, and dispersion parameters:
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\theta}
    = \{\alpha,\beta_f,\beta_L,\mathbf{a},
       \sigma,\sigma_{\mathrm{mat}}\}.
\end{equation}
Assuming conditional independence between bridge records, the likelihood of the observed log damping ratios is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})
    =
    \prod_{i\in\mathcal{O}}
    \mathcal{N}\!\left(
        \log\xi_i\mid\eta_i,\sigma^2
    \right).
\end{equation}
The posterior distribution follows from Bayes' rule:
\begin{equation}
    p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}})
    \propto
    \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})
    p(\boldsymbol{\theta}).
\end{equation}
For a bridge with unreported damping, its known predictors were used to obtain the posterior predictive distribution:
\begin{equation}
    p(\xi_i\mid\mathbf{x}_i,\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}})
    =
    \int
    p(\xi_i\mid\mathbf{x}_i,\boldsymbol{\theta})
    p(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}})
    \,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta},
    \qquad i\in\mathcal{M}.
\end{equation}
This distribution accounts for both uncertainty in the model parameters and the remaining variability between bridges. 
Samples from these distributions were used to represent the missing damping ratios in the subsequent population analysis.

\subsection{Bayesian estimation of pedestrian-to-structure
mass-ratio probabilities}\label{sec:mass_ratio_model}

The pedestrian-to-structure mass ratio depends on both the bridge modal properties and the distribution of pedestrians.
The present analysis therefore considers the prevalence of mass ratios at a specified pedestrian density $\rho$.

For bridge $i$, assuming pedestrians are uniformly distributed over the occupied deck area $A_i$, the pedestrian modal mass associated with the first vertical mode is
\begin{equation}
    M_{p,i}^{*}(\rho)
    =
    \rho\,\bar m_p
    \int_{A_i}\phi_i^2(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}A,
\end{equation}
where $\bar m_p$ is the assumed mean pedestrian mass and $\phi_i$ is the corresponding mode shape. 
The pedestrian-to-structure modal mass ratio is then,
\begin{equation}
    \mu_i(\rho)
    =
    \frac{M_{p,i}^{*}(\rho)}{M_{b,i}^{*}},
\end{equation}
where $M_{b,i}^{*}$ is the bridge modal mass evaluated using the same mode-shape $\phi_i$.

Sufficient information to calculate modal mass ratios is available for only a subset of bridges in the database.
A Bayesian model was therefore used to estimate the proportion of bridges whose mass ratios fall below a specified threshold $T$, without estimating a missing mass ratio for each bridge.
Threshold probabilities were estimated separately for each material category and then combined according to the estimated prevalence of these categories.
The probabilistic structure of the Bayesian mass-ratio model is shown in
\Cref{fig:mass_ratio_model}.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/fig_mass_ratio_bayesian_model.pdf}
    \caption{Probabilistic structure of the Bayesian model for pedestrian-to-structure mass-ratio probabilities.}
    \label{fig:mass_ratio_model}
\end{figure}

For a material catagory $m$, let $\theta_m(T;\rho)$ be,
\begin{equation}
    \theta_m(T;\rho)
    =
    P\bigl(\mu(\rho)<T\mid m\bigr).
\end{equation}
Let $n_m$ be the number of bridges with calculable mass ratios in category $m$, and let $k_m(T;\rho)$ be the number of these bridges whose mass ratios are below the threshold $T$.
The first likelihood  related these counts to the material specific threshold probability:
\begin{equation}
    k_m(T;\rho)
    \sim \operatorname{Binomial}
    \bigl(n_m,\theta_m(T;\rho)\bigr),
\end{equation}
This formulation assumes that bridges with calculable mass ratios are representative of their material category with respect to mass ratio.

The second likelyhood describes the material composition of the full database.
Let $N_m$ be the total number of bridges in material category $m$, including those without calculable mass ratios, and let $w_m=P(m)$ denote the corresponding population proportion.
For $M$ mutually exclusive and exhaustive material categories,
\begin{equation}
    N_{mat}\mid{w}
    \sim
    \operatorname{Multinomial}(N,{w}),
\end{equation}
where $N_{mat}=(N_1,\ldots,N_M)$, ${w}=(w_1,\ldots,w_M)$,
$N=\sum_{m=1}^{M}N_m$, and $\sum_{m=1}^{M}w_m=1$.
Consequently, all bridges contribute to estimating the material proportions, while only bridges with calculable mass ratios contribute to estimating the material-specific threshold probabilities.
The prior distributions for these parameters are specified in the following subsection.

The material proportions and threshold probabilities were combined using the law of total probability:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
    P\bigl(\mu(\rho)<T\bigr)
    &=
    \sum_{m=1}^{M}
    P(m)\,P\bigl(\mu(\rho)<T\mid m\bigr) \\
    &=
    \sum_{m=1}^{M}
    w_m\,\theta_m(T;\rho).
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:mass_ratio_probability}
\end{equation}
Each product gives the probability that a bridge belongs to material category $m$ and has a mass ratio below $T$.
Summing across the material categories give the overall proportion of bridges below the threshold.

To propagate uncertainty in both components, the overall probability was evaluated for each posterior sample $s$:
\begin{equation}
    F_{\mu}^{(s)}(T;\rho)
    =
    \sum_{m=1}^{M}
    w_m^{(s)}\,\theta_m^{(s)}(T;\rho).
\end{equation}
The resulting samples account for uncertainty in both the material proportions and the material-specific threshold probabilities.

The calculation was repeated over a range of thresholds $T$ at a specified pedestrian density $\rho$ to characterise the cumulative distribution of pedestrian-to-structure mass ratio. 
At each threshold, the posterior mean and 95\% creadible interval were calculated for $P\bigl(\mu(\rho)<T\bigr)$.
Lower and upper bound of these pointwise creadble intervals define the bounding curves of a probability box (p-box), allowing the estrimated proportion of bridges below any selected mass ratio threshold to be assessed together with its uncertianty.

\subsection{Prior specification and posterior computation}
% Prior distributions, software, sampling algorithm and settings.
As described in the \Cref{sec:damping_model}, the damping model contains 12 unknown model parameters, including the intercept $\alpha$, two regression coefficients $\beta_f$ and $\beta_L$, the residual standard deviation $\sigma$, and the between-material standard deviation $\sigma_{\mathrm{mat}}$.
The remaining seven parameters are the material-specific effects $a_m$, corresponding to the seven material categories represented in the database: steel, concrete, composite, fibre-reinforced polymer (FRP), aluminium, timber, and other materials.
Unreported damping ratios are additional latent quantitieswhose distributions are inferred from the model.

The intercept and regression coefficients were assigned normal priors:
\begin{equation}
    \alpha \sim \mathcal{N}\bigl(\log(0.01),0.5^2\bigr),
    \qquad
    \beta_f,\beta_L \sim \mathcal{N}(0,0.25^2),
\end{equation}
where the second argument denotes the variance.
The prior for $\alpha$ was centred at $\log(0.01)$, corresponding to a baseline damping ratio of 1\%. 
This value was selected to place the prior within the range commonly associated with footbridge vibration serviceability, where damping ratios are generally between 0.4\% to 2\% \cite{EN1991_2_2023,setra2006,feldman2008hivoss} for vibrations under pedestrian excitaions and vary with construction material.
The relatively broad prior on the logarithmic scale allows the observations and material specific effects to shift the baseline towards both lower and higher values.

The regression coefficients were assigned zero-mean priors, reflecting the absence of prior knowledge about the direction of their effects and to avoid favouring any particular relationship between damping and the predictors.
Because the log-transformed predictors were standardised, the same prior scale was used for both coefficients, regularising large effects while allowing the observations to inform their direction and magnitude.

The material-specific effects were assigned a shared normal prior,
\begin{equation}
    a_m \mid \sigma_{\mathrm{mat}}
    \sim
    \mathcal{N}\bigl(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{mat}}^2\bigr).
\end{equation}
This prior treats the material effects as deviations from the common regression relationship, centered at zero.
Sharing the same distribution across material categories allows partial pooling, so that material categories with few observations can borrow information from those with more data, while still allowing for differences between categories.
The between-material standard deviation controls the extent of variation among the material effects and was assigned the prior
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\mathrm{mat}}
    \sim
    \operatorname{Exponential}(6).
\end{equation}
An exponential prior was used because $\sigma_{\mathrm{mat}}$ is a
positive scale parameter.
This prior restricts $\sigma_{\mathrm{mat}}$ to positive values and places greater prior probability on relatively small differences between material categories while retaining support for larger differences if required by the data.

The remaining bridge-to-bridge variability not explained by the predictors
or material effects is represented by the residual standard deviation
$\sigma$, for which the prior
\begin{equation}
    \sigma \sim \operatorname{HalfNormal}(0.3)
\end{equation}
was adopted.
The half-normal prior similarly restricts the residual standard deviation to positive values while allowing substantial unexplained variability between bridges.

For computation, the material effects were expressed using a non-centred parameterisation with a sum-to-zero constraint,
\begin{equation}
    z_m \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1),
    \qquad
    \bar z = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M} z_j,
    \qquad
    a_m
    =
    \sigma_{\mathrm{mat}}
    \left(z_m-\bar z\right).
\end{equation}
The centring ensures that $\sum_{m=1}^{M} a_m = 0$, so that the material effects represent deviations from the common intercept $\alpha$.

For the mass-ratio model described in \Cref{sec:mass_ratio_model}, prior distributions were required for the material proportions $w_m$ and the material-specific threshold probabilities $\theta_m(T;\rho)$.
The material proportions were assigned a symmetric Dirichlet prior,
\begin{equation}
    w
    \sim
    \operatorname{Dirichlet}(1,\ldots,1),
\end{equation}
where the number of concentration parameters corresponds to the number of material categories considered in the mass-ratio analysis.
This prior treats the material categories symmetrically and does not favour any particular composition of the bridge population before observing the material counts in the database.

The material-specific threshold probabilities were assigned independent uniform beta priors,
\begin{equation}
    \theta_m(T;\rho)
    \sim
    \operatorname{Beta}(1,1).
\end{equation}
This prior assigns equal density to all probabilities between zero and one and therefore does not favour a particular probability that the mass ratio falls below the selected threshold within any material category.
The same prior specification for $\theta_m(T;\rho)$ was applied independently at each threshold $T$, whereas the material proportions $w$ describe the common material composition of the database and are therefore unchanged across thresholds.

Both Bayesian models were implemented in PyMC and posterior sampling was performed using the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS), an adaptive Hamiltonian Monte Carlo algorithm. 
For each model, four independent chains were run with 2000 tuning iterations followed by 2000 retained posterior samples per chain, using a target acceptance probability of 95\%.

\subsubsection{Vertical damping model}
The vertical damping model was assessed through prior predictive checks, sampling diagnostics, comparison of prior and posterior parameter distributions, and posterior predictive checks.
Prior predictive sampling was first used to examine whether the specified priors produced plausible damping ratios over the range of bridge characteristics represented in the database.
\Cref{fig:prior_predictive_check_damping} compares the resulting prior predictive PDF and CDF with the observed damping ratios.
As shown in \Cref{fig:prior_predictive_check_damping}(a), the prior predictive distribution is concentrated in the low-damping region, where most of the observations occur, while retaining support over the upper range of the observed damping ratios. 
This is also evident from \Cref{fig:prior_predictive_check_damping}(b), where the observed empirical CDF remains within the 95\% prior predictive interval over the considered range.
No substantial prior--data conflict was identified. 
The adopted priors were therefore considered sufficiently broad to represent plausible damping behaviour without placing excessive probability on unrealistically large damping ratios.

These diagnostics indicated satisfactory convergence and effective exploration of the posterior distribution.
